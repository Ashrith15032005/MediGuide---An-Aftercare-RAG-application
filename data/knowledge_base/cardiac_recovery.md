# Cardiac Recovery

## Overview
This guide covers recovery at home after a heart attack, a stent or angioplasty, or heart surgery such as bypass. Recovery is gradual, and a supervised cardiac rehabilitation program is one of the most effective ways to recover safely and lower the chance of another event. The plan in your discharge summary and from your cardiologist always comes first; this guide gives general information.

## Activity and Walking
Start with short, slow walks on flat ground, several times a day, and increase gradually as your team advises. Rest between activities. You should be able to talk while walking. Stop and rest if you feel unusually tired or uncomfortable, and do not push through symptoms. Avoid heavy lifting, pushing or pulling (often nothing heavier than about 2 to 4 kilograms, or a full kettle, at first) until your team clears you. After heart surgery through the breastbone, avoid pushing up with your arms from a chair or bed, and hold a small pillow against your chest when you cough or sneeze, for about six to eight weeks or as advised. After a stent through the wrist or groin, avoid heavy use of that arm or leg for the period your team specified.

## Cardiac Rehabilitation
Cardiac rehabilitation combines supervised exercise, education, diet advice and emotional support, usually over several weeks. Ask your cardiologist for a referral if you have not been offered one. It is safe because your heart rate, blood pressure and symptoms are monitored while you build up your fitness.

## Heart-Healthy Diet
Choose vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish and low-fat dairy. Limit saturated fat (ghee, butter, fatty meat, fried foods), trans fat (some packaged and bakery foods), and processed meats. Use vegetable oils such as olive, mustard or groundnut oil in small amounts, and bake, steam, grill or boil instead of deep frying. Reduce salt to less than about 5 grams (one level teaspoon) per day, and limit pickles, papad, chips and packaged foods. Limit sugar and sugary drinks. Avoid alcohol or keep it minimal as your doctor advises. Ask about fluid limits if you have heart failure.

## Medicines
Take every heart medicine exactly as prescribed. After a stent, antiplatelet tablets prevent clots from forming in the stent, and stopping them early can be dangerous. Never stop, skip or change a heart medicine on your own, even if you feel well. Use a pill organizer or phone reminders. Carry a current list of your medicines. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking over-the-counter pain relievers, cold remedies or supplements.

## Wound and Access-Site Care
For a chest incision, keep it clean and dry, shower as allowed, pat dry, and do not use creams or powders on it unless advised. For a wrist or groin puncture site, keep it clean and watch it as instructed. Do not soak in a bath or swimming pool until healed.

## Driving, Travel and Returning to Work
Driving is usually restricted for a period that depends on your procedure, often about one week after a stent and four to six weeks after open surgery, and it can differ after a heart attack. Ask your cardiologist before you drive or fly. Many people return to desk work after several weeks; a physical job may take longer.

## Intimacy
Many people can resume sexual activity when they can climb two flights of stairs comfortably, but ask your cardiologist first, especially if you take medicines for chest pain.

## Emotional Wellbeing
Feeling anxious, low or tearful after a heart event is common. Talk about it with your family and care team, and ask about cardiac rehabilitation programs that include emotional support. Do not ignore low mood that lasts more than a couple of weeks.

## Smoking, Weight and Sleep
Stopping smoking is one of the most important steps you can take; ask your doctor about support and medicines to help you quit. Work toward a healthy weight gradually. Aim for 7 to 8 hours of sleep. Stress management such as relaxation breathing, walking and hobbies helps.

## Home Monitoring
Weigh yourself each morning at the same time if you have heart failure, and write it down. Check blood pressure and pulse as your doctor advised and keep a log. Bring the log to appointments.

## Follow-up
Follow-up visits are usually at one to two weeks, then at one to three months, and then as your cardiologist directs. Blood tests for cholesterol and other markers are repeated over time. Keep all appointments and bring your medicine list.

## Warning Signs
- Chest pain, pressure, tightness or pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back (call emergency services)
- Sudden shortness of breath, or breathlessness at rest or lying flat
- Fainting, near fainting, or a rapid or very irregular heartbeat
- Sudden weakness, face drooping or trouble speaking (call emergency services)
- Weight gain of more than 2 kilograms in a few days, or new swelling of the legs or abdomen
- Redness, swelling, pus or fluid from a chest incision, wrist or groin site, or a chest that feels like it is clicking or moving
- Bleeding from the access site that does not stop with pressure
- Fever of 38 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) or higher
