# Appendectomy Recovery

## Overview
An appendectomy is surgery to remove the appendix, done either through several small cuts (laparoscopic) or one larger cut (open). Recovery is usually quicker after laparoscopic surgery. Most people return to light daily activity within one to two weeks and to full activity within about four to six weeks, but the exact timeline depends on the type of surgery and whether the appendix had burst. The instructions in your own discharge summary always come first; this guide gives general information.

## Wound Care
Keep the dressings clean and dry. Ask your surgical team when dressings can come off; many small dressings are removed after one to two days. Once cleared, you can usually shower and let soapy water run over the wounds, then pat them dry. Do not scrub the wounds, and do not soak in a bath, swimming pool or hot tub until the wounds are fully closed and your surgeon agrees. Do not apply creams, powders or home remedies to the wounds unless your surgeon advised it. Skin glue or adhesive strips will fall off on their own; do not pick at them.

## Activity and Rest
Walk short distances several times a day from the first day at home. Walking helps circulation and bowel recovery. Increase the distance gradually. Avoid lifting anything heavier than about 4 to 5 kilograms (a full kettle or a gallon of milk is around 4 kg) for about two weeks after laparoscopic surgery and about four to six weeks after open surgery, unless your surgeon says otherwise. Avoid strenuous exercise, sit-ups and contact sports until you are cleared at follow-up. Support your abdomen with a small pillow when you cough, laugh or get out of bed.

## Driving and Returning to Work
Do not drive while you are taking prescription pain medicine or if turning quickly or braking hard causes discomfort in the abdomen. Many people are comfortable driving after about one to two weeks. Desk work can often restart after one to two weeks; physical jobs usually need longer. Ask your surgeon for a written clearance if your job is physical.

## Diet and Hydration
Start with light foods and build up to your normal diet as your appetite returns. Good early choices include rice, plain khichdi, dal soup, toast, bananas, curd or yogurt, boiled vegetables and clear soups. Eat small meals more often instead of two or three large ones. Drink plenty of fluids, about 8 glasses of water a day unless you were told to limit fluids. Fiber from fruit, vegetables and whole grains helps prevent constipation, which is common after surgery and after taking pain medicine. Limit very spicy, oily and fried foods for the first week or two if they upset your stomach. Avoid alcohol while you are taking prescription pain medicine or antibiotics.

## Bowel Habits
Constipation is common after abdominal surgery. Walking, fluids and fiber all help. Do not strain. Your doctor may recommend a stool softener; take only what your doctor or pharmacist recommends.

## Pain and Medicines
Take medicines exactly as they were prescribed, at the times listed on your prescription. Finish the full antibiotic course if one was prescribed. Taking pain medicine with food can protect the stomach. Keep a written list of all your medicines and bring it to follow-up visits. Questions about changing, skipping or combining medicines should go to your doctor or pharmacist.

## Sleep and Comfort
Sleep with a pillow under your knees or lie on your back or side, whichever is more comfortable. Loose, soft clothing that does not press on the wounds is more comfortable than a tight waistband.

## Follow-up
Most people have a check-up with the surgeon about one to two weeks after surgery. Keep this appointment even if you feel well. If a pathology report was taken, ask for the result at this visit. Bring your discharge summary and medicine list.

## Warning Signs
- Fever of 38 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) or higher, or chills
- Worsening or new severe abdominal pain, or a swollen, hard abdomen
- Redness, swelling, warmth, pus or bleeding from a wound, or a wound that opens
- Repeated vomiting or being unable to keep fluids down
- No bowel movement or passing gas for more than three days, or persistent diarrhea
- Pain, swelling or redness in a calf, or sudden shortness of breath or chest pain (seek emergency care)
- Burning when passing urine or being unable to pass urine
