# C-Section Recovery

## Overview
A caesarean section (C-section) is a delivery through a cut in the lower abdomen and womb. It is major abdominal surgery, so recovery takes longer than many people expect: usually four to six weeks for the incision to heal well, with energy returning over several more weeks. Rest when the baby sleeps, accept help with housework, and follow the instructions in your discharge summary, which come first over any general guide.

## Wound Care
Keep the incision clean and dry. You can usually shower once your care team allows it; let water run over the incision, do not scrub, and pat it dry with a clean towel. Wear loose, soft cotton underwear and clothing with a high waist that does not rub the incision. Do not soak in a bath or swimming pool until the incision is completely healed and your doctor agrees. Skin glue or adhesive strips fall off on their own. Do not apply creams, oils or home remedies to the incision unless your doctor advised it. If the skin folds over the incision, keep the area dry and clean.

## Activity and Rest
Walk short distances several times a day from the first day. Increase gradually. Avoid lifting anything heavier than your baby for the first six weeks, and avoid vigorous exercise, abdominal exercises and heavy housework until your doctor clears you at the follow-up visit. Climbing stairs slowly is fine. When you get out of bed, roll to your side and push up with your arms instead of sitting straight up. Hold a pillow firmly over the incision when you cough, sneeze or laugh.

## Driving
Do not drive until you can brake suddenly and turn to look over your shoulder without pain, and you are not taking prescription pain medicine. This is often around two weeks after surgery, but follow your doctor's advice and check your insurance rules.

## Breastfeeding and Positions
You can breastfeed after a C-section. Comfortable positions that keep the baby's weight off the incision include the side-lying position and the football (clutch) hold, with a pillow supporting the baby. Ask your nurse, midwife or lactation consultant for help if positioning is difficult.

## Diet and Hydration
Eat a balanced diet with protein, fruit, vegetables and whole grains to support healing and milk production. Drink plenty of water, about 8 to 10 glasses a day, more if you are breastfeeding. Fiber and fluids help prevent constipation, which is common after surgery and after taking iron tablets or pain medicine. Small frequent meals are often easier at first. Limit caffeine if you are breastfeeding and it affects the baby's sleep. Avoid alcohol while taking prescription pain medicine.

## Bowel and Bladder
Constipation and gas pains are common in the first days. Walking, fluids and fiber help. Do not strain; supporting the incision with a pillow can make bowel movements more comfortable. Your doctor may recommend a stool softener.

## Pain and Medicines
Take pain medicine, iron or calcium supplements and any other medicines exactly as prescribed, at the listed times. Taking pain medicine before the pain becomes strong, as your doctor advised, keeps you comfortable enough to move and care for the baby. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before combining any medicine while breastfeeding.

## Emotional Wellbeing and Sleep
Tiredness, mood swings and tearfulness in the first two weeks are common. Rest in short blocks, share night feeds if you can, and accept help. If low mood lasts longer than two weeks or you feel unable to cope, talk to your doctor or midwife.

## Intimacy and Contraception
Most doctors advise waiting about six weeks and until bleeding has settled and you feel comfortable. Discuss contraception at your follow-up visit, since fertility can return before periods restart.

## Follow-up
You will usually have a check with your doctor around two weeks for wound review and again around six weeks. Keep these appointments. Bring your discharge summary, medicine list and any questions about feeding, contraception and returning to exercise.

## Warning Signs
- Fever of 38 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) or higher, or chills
- Redness, swelling, warmth, pus or increasing pain at the incision, or a wound that opens
- Heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking a pad in an hour) or passing large clots, or foul-smelling discharge
- Severe or worsening abdominal pain
- Pain, swelling or redness in a calf, or sudden shortness of breath or chest pain (seek emergency care)
- Burning when passing urine, or difficulty passing urine
- Red, hard, painful area in a breast with fever
- Feeling hopeless, or thoughts of harming yourself or the baby (seek help immediately)
